Boundary compass · design notes

Complete separation. The physical tree answers where an item lives and nothing else: no relation lines, no counts, no external pseudo-folders. Selecting an item raises a separate bridge layer that answers what reaches it, what it asks for, and which side of the bundle boundary each answer lands on. The two surfaces only meet through explicit commands: Locate (show me where this is) and Re-center (make this the selection).

  • North/south is direction. The spine runs through the selection. Ingress lanes join it from the north, egress lanes leave it to the south.
  • West/east is the bundle boundary. Every lane's endpoint sits on the side where its resolution lands. Internal bundle resolution therefore reads as south, then east; anything leaving the app reads as south, then west and visibly crosses the dashed Atlas.app boundary.
  • The bridge carries the request, not the target. The plank on the spine is the recorded reference (load name, string, key, seal entry). The endpoint is what it resolves to in the current context. Ticks on a lane count nested bundle (code-unit) boundaries it crosses.
  • Routes unfold toward their side. Opening a lane shows the step-by-step resolution route (runpath probes, link hops, base assumptions) in the half of the layer where it ends.
  • Context is first-class. Layout (original vs staged copy) and slice change which side a lane lands on. The relocation failure from the rebuilding design becomes visible: an absolute load name that lands east in the original flips west in the staged copy, and the bypassed staged framework shows a dashed ghost lane.
  • Bundles are boundaries too. Selecting a bundle or folder aggregates its traffic: only lanes that cross its edge are drawn; internal relations collapse into a count.

Walkthrough

  1. Example (framework executable) is selected. Open Render Helper's lane: two runpath probes, two boundary ticks.
  2. Switch Layout to Staged copy. Atlas's lane becomes a bypass ghost.
  3. Re-center on Atlas from that lane. The same request now sits on the south bridge and exits west.
  4. Re-center on libSystem.B.dylib to see every requester inside the app.
  5. Select Example.framework, Render Helper.app or _CodeSignature/CodeResources to compare aggregate scope and a seal's fan-out.
  6. Switch Slice to x86_64: the arm64-only helper lanes disappear.
  7. Hide the tree (T) and navigate using breadcrumbs, membrane labels and Re-center only.

Compared with the other studies

  • Mixed relation overlay draws numbered lines in a gutter beside the tree. Here the tree stays clean, external references leave the tree entirely, and lanes never cross each other because each relation is one row.
  • Card-based bridge layouts place incoming and outgoing cards around the selection with a resolution panel to the side. Here east and west encode the boundary itself, so "does this stay inside the copy?" is answered by position before reading any text.
  • The row layout grows linearly and can be virtualized; a fan-out such as a resource seal stays readable.

What to evaluate

  • Does reading position (W/E) before text speed up staging questions?
  • Are boundary ticks understandable without hovering, or is the endpoint's text doing the work?
  • Is the separation comfortable, or do Locate and Re-center feel like too many hops?
  • Should a second hop (ingress of ingress, egress of egress) fold out north and south?

Limits

The tree, relations, routes and status labels are fixed illustrative data written for this design conversation. Routes are authored, not computed: nothing here resolves runpaths, parses Mach-O, reads seals or inspects a filesystem. "Present" means represented in the example inventory. A candidate is not a loaded library, a seal entry is not an integrity check, a declared entitlement is not observed access, and crossing a bundle boundary says nothing about signature validity. Any product version must receive these facts and relationships from their ordinary Rust owners.
